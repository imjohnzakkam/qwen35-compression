Blaze: put the bits where the model burns them

A 4-bit Qwen3.5 checkpoint spends 27–53% of its bytes on a BF16 vocabulary table that mostly sits idle. Blaze moves those bytes into the transformer body, with no Fisher weighting, no training and no in-domain calibration answers. Measured on the 4B at a standard 4-bit export's size, it now drifts 8.5× less than Glaze v2 and matches BF16 on the benchmark panel.

Draft October 15, 2026 · results updated October 16, 2026 · Qwen3.5-4B and 2B, instruct mode, greedy decoding · measured numbers come from this repo; predictions are labeled

TL;DR

Blaze stops tuning 4-bit rounding and moves bytes instead.

Why not better rounding
WaterSIC proves per-layer quantization can get within 0.25 bit of its limit, and rotated GPTQ lands within 0.1 bit of WaterSIC. Glaze v2's drift gain is about what its extra 0.7 bit per weight predicts.
Where the bytes are
The vocabulary table is 34% of Glaze v2's 3.77 GB. 68% of its 248,320 rows never appeared in 57M tokens of English text and model output.
The method
A usage ladder (busy rows 8-bit, the rest 4-bit), a byte market that prices every body unit by measured KL, and stock Marlin kernels.
What was predicted
On the 4B at a 4-bit export's size: excess loss of 0.0011–0.002 with a 6-bit option, 7–13× below Glaze v2's 0.014, or about 1.1–1.4% flips against 3.29%.
What was measured
On the 4B at W4A16's size: excess loss 0.0017 and 1.39% flips, inside the prediction, and every panel task within a point of BF16. On the 2B: 0.0005 against standard W4A16's 0.073, served for real by vLLM at W4A16's size.
Cost so far
₹267.84 (about $2.78) for seven A30 runs. G4, the 4B's full suite, is running, capped at ₹340.

Where we started

Glaze v2 has closed most of the benchmark gap, so what is left is token-level drift. All numbers are this repo's Qwen3.5-4B records: instruct mode, greedy decoding, 8,192-token cap.

  • Drift. On BF16's 1,501 replayed answers (2.3M tokens), Glaze v2 picks a different top token 3.29% of the time; the noise floor is 0.35%. Its excess loss is 0.014 nats per token, against INT8 W8A8's 0.011 and AutoRound's 0.042.
  • Drift by domain. Glaze v2 flips 2.1% of tokens on MATH-500 answers and 3.8% on MMLU-Pro answers. The in-domain calibration shows.
  • Where the gain came from. The data control credits 7.0 of Glaze v2's 10.0 MATH-500 points over AutoRound to its in-domain calibration data. Its own stages add about 3.

The target is drift, not benchmarks: excess loss from 0.014 toward 0, without in-domain data. A method that needs Glaze v2's calibration set to get there has only redone Glaze.

Observations

Six facts, four of them measured on this repo's checkpoints, point the same way: the next large gain is in the byte budget, not in the rounding.

Observation 1Inside a layer, 4-bit quantization is already near its limit

WaterSIC is a per-layer quantizer proven to sit within 0.25 bit per weight of the information-theoretic limit [1]. On Llama-3-8B, plain GPTQ with a random rotation lands within 0.1 bit of WaterSIC [2]. Glaze's Fisher-weighted rounding competes for that last tenth of a bit; vector codes such as QTIP [3] compete for the 0.25.

Observation 2In this repo, extra bits explain Glaze v2's drift gain

Quantization noise power falls about 4× per added bit. From AutoRound's 0.042 excess loss at 4.12 bits per body weight, that rule predicts 0.016 at Glaze v2's 4.83 bits; Glaze v2 measured 0.014. Two points are not a law, but they say the bytes Glaze v2 took from the vision tower did most of the work.

Observation 3The vocabulary is the largest byte pool in small Qwen3.5 exports

Every Qwen3.5 size shares one 248,320-token vocabulary. In a standard W4A16 export (4-bit body, BF16 vocabulary and vision tower), its tables are 27–53% of the file. The 0.8B, 2B and 4B tie the table to the LM head, so all of it is read for every generated token.

Observation 4Most of the vocabulary sits idle

In 57M tokens (ten cached English datasets plus 22M tokens the BF16 4B generated itself), only 80,300 of 248,320 rows ever appear. The other 68% never do, and frequencies follow Zipf's law with a slope of −1.10. The 96,677 rows added since Qwen3's vocabulary carry 0.2–0.3% of tokens.

Idle depends on the domain, though. A ranking built only from chat and Wikipedia text leaves 7.8% of the model's own math and multiple-choice output tokens outside its top 32,768 rows, and 1.6% outside 65,536. Tiers need broad data and a precision floor, not a hard cut.

Observation 5Idle rows are cheap to store, but must still be stored well

Row norms are flat across usage (median 0.63 for the busiest 32k rows, 0.65 for never-seen rows), so a badly stored rare row can still win the argmax. Entries are near-Gaussian (kurtosis 3.0–3.4) and 256 directions hold only 30–36% of the energy, so per-row scalar grids fit and low-rank tricks do not.

Observation 6Structure cannot be bolted on after training

The Hadamard MLP trains Kronecker-structured channel mixers from scratch and still gives up about 18% in loss [12]. Post hoc it is hopeless. Blaze uses Hadamard transforms only as exact rotations, never as replacements.

The Blaze method

Blaze treats the whole checkpoint as one byte budget and puts the bits where the model burns them. It takes bytes from idle vocabulary rows, prices every body unit by measuring what an extra bit buys, and buys bits until the budget is spent.

Stage 1Usage census, on CPU, once per tokenizer

Count how often each of the 248,320 rows appears as an input in a broad corpus (web, code, math, chat, several languages), and how often the model emits it in its own generations. Every Qwen3.5 size shares the tokenizer, so one census serves the family.

Stage 2Vocabulary ladder

Renumber the rows by use. The busiest rows, enough to cover 99.9% of census tokens and never fewer than 65,536, are stored at 8 bits with group 128; every other row at 4 bits.

  • Tied models (0.8B to 4B): one laddered table serves both the input lookup and the LM head.
  • Untied models (9B and up): the head gets the ladder. The input table, read one row per token, goes to 4 bits throughout.
  • Why these rungs: an 8-bit row adds about 0.014 nats of logit noise and a 4-bit row about 0.26 (observation 5).

Stage 3Body menu

Each unit that vLLM runs as one kernel (q/k/v, gate/up, DeltaNet's input projections, and the rest) takes one option: 4 bits with group 128, 64 or 32, or 8 bits with group 128. All of them run on stock Marlin kernels. GPTQ quantizes the 4-bit units and round-to-nearest the 8-bit ones.

Stage 4Measured byte market

For each unit and option, quantize that unit alone in an otherwise BF16 model and measure how much KL to BF16 it removes, on generic replay text. The drift study found that per-component excess losses add up (to 101% of the whole), so these prices can be summed. An exact multiple-choice knapsack, solved over 64 KiB bins with Glaze v2's solver, buys the options with the most KL removed per byte.

Stage 5Export and serving

The body ships as compressed-tensors config groups, as Glaze v2 does. The vocabulary ships as a tiered module: rows stored by rung in renumbered order, plus a 248,320-entry index map. The LM head runs one Marlin GEMM per rung into a single logits buffer, and token IDs map back before detokenizing.

Stage 6 · stretchA 6-bit kernel

A 4/8-bit menu is blunt: 8 bits is more than most units need, and the 4-bit remainder keeps most of the error. A W6A16 kernel, either an INT6 variant of Marlin or FP6 through FP6-LLM's tensor-core kernels [11], would let the body sit near-uniformly at 6–7 bits.

# Blaze for one checkpoint at byte budget B
# 1-2: census once per tokenizer, then the ladder
census = count_inputs(broad_corpus) + count_outputs(model_generations)
hot    = busiest_rows(census, cover=0.999, at_least=65_536)
vocab  = ladder(renumber(census), hot=int8_g128, rest=int4_g128)

# 3-4: price every option by measured KL, then buy
for unit in fusion_units(model):        # q/k/v, gate/up, ...
    for opt in [int4_g64, int4_g32, int8_g128]:
        base = kl(bf16, swap(unit, int4_g128))
        gain[unit, opt] = base - kl(bf16, swap(unit, opt))
left   = B - bytes(vocab) - bytes(vision_int8) - bytes(norms)
choice = knapsack(gain, bytes_of, budget=left)

# 5: GPTQ for 4-bit units, round-to-nearest for 8-bit
export(quantize(model, choice), vocab)

Two operating points. Blaze-Q spends the freed bytes on quality at Glaze v2's 3.77 GB, which leaves about 6.8 bits per body weight. Blaze-S keeps Glaze v2's 4.83 bits per body weight and banks the bytes: about 2.91 GB, 23% smaller.

Left out on purpose: Fisher or Hessian weighting in the objective, learned rounding, training toward the teacher, and in-domain calibration answers. The teacher only prices options, on generic text. Blaze's one second-order step is GPTQ's own per-layer solve, which WaterSIC shows is near-optimal.

Why it should close the gap

On the 4B, Blaze-Q should cut Glaze v2's excess loss 7–13× once a 6-bit option exists, and 1.2–3.6× on today's 4/8-bit kernels. Every input below is measured in this repo or derived from the weights; the outputs are predictions, not results.

Error model

Excess loss adds across units (the drift study's 101%), and each unit's share falls 4× per added bit:

L≈∑usu2−2bu

At a uniform 4.12 bits this is AutoRound's 0.042. At a uniform 6.8 bits it is 0.042 × 2−5.3 ≈ 0.0011, and any allocation smarter than uniform only lowers it. That is the 6-bit case, and it holds whatever the sensitivity profile.

The menu caveat. With only 4- and 8-bit kernels, Blaze-Q can put about 64% of body weights at 8 bits; the 4-bit rest keeps its full error. If all FFN layers were equally sensitive, that rest would hold about 30% of the sensitivity and L ≈ 0.012, barely under Glaze v2. If sensitivity is steep, L falls toward 0.004. Glaze v2's knapsack put 27 of its 40 8-bit picks in layers 0–7, which hints at steep, but that was a Fisher prediction; gate G1 measures it.

From loss to flips. Across this repo's five weight-only points (BF16, Glaze v2, AutoRound, AWQ, GPTQ), the flip rate fits within 0.2 points of

flips≈0.35%+23×L

So L of 0.0011–0.002 means about 1.1–1.4% flips, and 0.004–0.012 means 1.8–2.9%. Glaze v2 sits at 3.29%, INT8 W8A8 at 2.42%. Measured: G2b reached L = 0.0017 with 1.39% flips; the formula gives 1.30%.

The ladder's own cost. Gaussian noise of σ nats on competing logits adds at most σ²/2 of KL. At 8 bits (σ ≈ 0.014) the busy rows cost about 0.0001 per token. Idle rows at 4 bits (σ ≈ 0.26) only matter when they come within about a nat of the top choice, which the census says is rare; gate G1 checks it.

Benchmarks. At L ≤ 0.002, every text task should sit within run-to-run noise of BF16 (1–2 points). MMMU is the open case: its −2.9 is shared by every 4-bit method and may come from image-token activations that text replay never prices.

Blaze-S keeps Glaze v2's 4.83 bits per body weight with generic data, so the same model predicts L ≈ 0.016, Glaze v2's level, at 23% fewer bytes.

Generality

The lever grows as models shrink and vocabularies grow, and every Qwen3.5 size can reuse one ladder because they share a tokenizer. At the size of a standard W4A16 export, Blaze can give the 0.8B and 2B an all-8-bit body and the 9B 8.0 bits per weight, which should make all three near-lossless on stock kernels.

  • Mixture of experts. In Qwen3.5-35B-A3B at batch 1, the BF16 head is 45% of the weight bytes read per token (1.02 GB against 1.25 GB of active 4-bit experts and mixers). There the ladder is mainly a speed lever.
  • Beyond Qwen. The same arithmetic on other small tied models gives vocabulary shares of 46% for Qwen3-1.7B, 51% for Llama-3.2-1B and 63% for Gemma-3-1B in a 4-bit export.
  • What transfers. The census and the ladder transfer to any model with the same tokenizer. The byte market is re-measured per model: about an hour of A30 time on the 4B, growing with size.
  • Architecture. Nothing in Blaze depends on DeltaNet. Units are whatever the serving engine fuses, so dense, hybrid and MoE layouts all fit.

Kernels and deployment

Blaze's quality claims need no new kernels. A fake-quantized export (the stored values, dequantized into BF16 tensors) produces the same outputs, so gates G1 to G3 run on stock vLLM; real kernels matter only for the size and speed claims.

Decode path with a laddered vocabulary:

  1. Input token IDs pass through a 248,320-entry index map (1 MB) into renumbered order.
  2. The embedding lookup reads one row from its rung and dequantizes it (8-bit or 4-bit, group 128).
  3. The body runs on stock Marlin kernels, as Glaze v2 does.
  4. The LM head runs one Marlin GEMM per rung, writing into adjacent slices of one logits buffer, with no gather or copy.
  5. Argmax or sampling runs in renumbered space, and the chosen ID maps back before detokenizing.

Inside vLLM. vLLM 0.29 gives Qwen3.5's embedding no quantization config, so the vocabulary needs a model patch. An out-of-tree model class registered with vLLM's model registry can swap in a tiered embedding and head and leave everything else stock. Renumbering is exact, so a unit test can demand bit-identical logits against the dequantized reference.

The 6-bit option. Two routes, both off the critical path until G1 shows they pay: FP6 (e3m2) through FP6-LLM's TC-FPx kernels, or an INT6 variant of Marlin's dequantize-in-registers design that keeps Blaze's integer grids. Either is a custom vLLM op. If neither lands, Blaze-Q on the 4B rests on the 4/8-bit menu, and the 0.8B, 2B and 9B results are unaffected.

Rotations. An exact residual-stream Hadamard rotation [4] would put GPTQ's 4-bit units within reach of WaterSIC's bound. Tied tables complicate it: folding the final norm into a rotated head unties it from the input table, which costs a second laddered table (about 0.41 GB on the 4B). Blaze therefore rotates only untied models by default.

Risks and kill criteria

Blaze dies early and cheaply if the ladder is not free (G1a), or if neither the 4/8-bit menu nor a 6-bit option gets the 4B at least 2× below Glaze v2 (G1b with G2). Either outcome is still worth a short write-up: the byte census alone changes how this repo should report "4-bit".

  • Idle rows matter more than the census says.LaTeX-heavy math or non-English prompts can lean on rows an English census calls idle. Mitigation: a multilingual, code-heavy census, a larger hot rung, and a floor that never goes below 4 bits.Kill: G1a fails after one retest at 131,072 hot rows
  • The 4/8-bit menu is too blunt on the 4B.If FFN sensitivity is flat across layers, Blaze-Q lands near Glaze v2. Mitigation: the 6-bit kernel, or 8-bit and 4-bit rungs inside each FFN, sorted by an exact neuron permutation.Signal: G1c and G2
  • Prices do not add up at unit granularity.The 101% additivity was measured on three components, not 152 units. Mitigation: re-price once at the chosen solution (about one more GPU-hour) and re-solve.
  • MMMU stays near −3.Text replay never prices image-token activations. Mitigation: add image-text replay to the market, or keep the vision tower in BF16 where bytes allow.
  • Serving drift.A model patch for the tiered vocabulary can break on vLLM upgrades, and the 6-bit kernel can slip. Quality gates run on fake-quant exports, so this delays only the size and speed claims.
  • "It's a budget trick."Reviewers compare at equal bits per Linear layer, where Blaze's body is plain GPTQ by design. Report both protocols, and give the strongest baseline the same ladder in control 2.
  • Speed at equal size.On the 9B, an all-8-bit body reads 35% more bytes per token than a W4A16 export. Blaze-Q trades decode speed for quality there; Blaze-S is the speed point.
  • Prior art.Lower precision for rarely used vocabulary rows is not new. The novelty claim must rest on equal-size reinvestment, one ladder for a whole family, and tied-table serving.

Related work and novelty

Each of Blaze's parts has a precedent; the combination, and where it looks for bytes, appears to be new. Searched arXiv and the web on 15 October 2026.

Not new: per-layer solvers, rotations, measured mixed precision, or giving rarely used vocabulary rows fewer bits.

New, as far as this search found:

  1. One byte market at fixed artifact size, in which vocabulary rows, the vision tower and body units are all priced by measured KL.
  2. One usage ladder reused across every model that shares a tokenizer.
  3. A tied table laddered once and served as both lookup and LM head on stock Marlin kernels.
  4. The argument, from WaterSIC and this repo's own drift points, that past about 4 bits the bytes, not the rounding, set the drift.
