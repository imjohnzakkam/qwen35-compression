Blaze-Q and Blaze-S Where Blaze stands Scorecard

Blaze technical report

Blaze is a training-free way to compress small language models that treats the checkpoint as one byte budget. A standard 4-bit export of Qwen3.5-4B spends a third of its file on a BF16 vocabulary table whose rows mostly never appear. Blaze stores that table by usage and spends the freed bytes on the transformer body. At the exact size of a GPTQ W4A16 file, the result drifts 30× less from BF16 and stays within a point of it on 12 of 13 benchmarks. In this report we share the measurements that led there, the method, every result at equal file size, how the model serves, and what did not work.

Where a 4-bit file's bytes go

Weight-only 4-bit quantization with group-wise scales (W4A16) is the default way to serve open models on one GPU, and new methods are judged by how much better they round each Linear layer at that width. That race is nearly run. WaterSIC quantizes a linear layer within 0.25 bit per weight of the information-theoretic limit, and GPTQ with a random rotation lands within 0.1 bit of WaterSIC.

A "4-bit" checkpoint is not four bits per weight, though. GPU serving formats (GPTQ, AWQ and AutoRound exports in vLLM) quantize the body's Linear layers and keep the vocabulary table and the vision tower in BF16. vLLM gives Qwen3.5's embedding no quantization option at all. Small models share a large 248,320-token vocabulary, so the table is the largest single object in the file. In the tied models it doubles as the LM head and is read in full for every generated token, 41% of the weight bytes in a 4B decode step.

The vocabulary is mostly idle

We counted token use in a 42.3M-token census of public training text and model answers: training splits of MATH, GSM8K, ARC-Challenge, SciQ, HellaSwag and WikiText-2, 20,000 UltraChat conversations, 20,000 COCO captions and 3.0M tokens of BF16 answers to training prompts. No benchmark question or answer is counted. Only 80,010 of the 248,320 rows ever appear, and their frequencies follow Zipf's law with a slope of −1.08.

Scored on held-out text, the busiest 65,536 rows cover 99.88% of the 19.1M tokens BF16 writes on benchmarks, and 96.5% of code. They cover only 26.5% of non-English text, because the census is English and other scripts live in its tail. Adding 300,000 tokens of FineWeb-2 in each of 30 languages lifts non-English coverage at the same 65,536 rows to 93.2%.

Idle rows still need care

Row norms are flat across usage: the median is 0.63 for the busiest 32k rows and 0.65 for rows never seen. So a badly stored rare row can still win the argmax. With a hidden-state norm of about 162 after the final norm's gain, a row quantized with grid step Δ adds logit noise σ = Δ‖h‖/√12: a median 0.014 nats at 8 bits and 0.26 at 4 bits. Noise of σ on competing logits costs at most σ²/2 in KL, under 10⁻⁴ per token at 8 bits. At 4 bits it matters only for rows near the top of the distribution, which is exactly where usage puts the 8-bit tier.

Bytes, not rounding, set the drift

We measure damage as drift from BF16 on BF16's own answers. The excess loss L is the extra negative log-likelihood per token a quantized model pays when it reads BF16's greedy answers to MATH-500 and MMLU-Pro (1,501 answers, 2.30M tokens). The flip rate is the share of positions where its top token differs from BF16's; BF16 replayed against itself flips 0.35%, the noise floor.

Across every weight-only checkpoint in this study, L falls about 4× per added bit of body precision, as quantization noise power should. AutoRound's 0.042 at 4.12 bits per body weight predicts 0.016 at 4.83 bits; an in-domain mixed-precision export at 4.83 bits measured 0.014. Rounding the whole 4B body to nearest at 4 and at 6 bits gives losses 19.5× apart, against 16× for the rule. If drift is set by bits, the question is where the next bit can come from: better rounding offers at most 0.10 bit per weight, vector codes at most 0.25, and the vocabulary holds 1.93.

Method

Blaze turns a BF16 checkpoint into one export of a chosen byte size in five stages. Nothing is trained, and no Fisher weighting, learned rounding or in-domain calibration data is used.

Usage census and ladder

Rows are renumbered by census frequency. The busiest 65,536 rows are stored as symmetric INT8 with one BF16 scale per group of 128 entries, and the other 182,784 rows at 4 bits with the same grouping. A 1 MB index map carries token IDs into tier order and back. For Qwen3.5-4B the table shrinks from 1,271 MB to 412 MB. Tied models ladder the table once and serve it as both lookup and LM head.

Units, menu and byte market

A unit is the set of Linear layers the serving engine fuses into one kernel call, so a precision choice never splits a fused projection. Qwen3.5-4B has 64: the token mixer (Gated DeltaNet or attention) and the FFN of each of its 32 layers. Each unit takes 4, 6 or 8 bits with group 128. Each unit and option is priced by the KL divergence to BF16 that quantizing that unit alone adds, measured on 16 blocks of 2,048 WikiText-2 training tokens. Unit errors add up almost exactly: in an earlier study on this model, the attention, DeltaNet and FFN components summed to 101% of the whole. So the market solves a multiple-choice knapsack at a fixed file size:

min{ou}∑upu(ou)s.t.∑ubytesu(ou)≤B−Bvocab−Bvision−Brest

The error model behind it is L ≈ Σu su 4−bu, with su a unit's sensitivity and bu its bits per weight. Prices measured directly at 6 bits were indistinguishable from noise (they summed to 0.048 against 0.197 at 4 bits, a factor of 4 where the model expects 16). So the market ranks units by their 4-bit prices, scaled by the measured 19.5× per two bits, and fills the budget upward from an all-6-bit body. Body Linears are then rounded by GPTQ on generic WikiText-2 text, and the vision tower is rounded to nearest at 8 bits.

Two operating points

  • Blaze-Q spends the freed bytes on the body at a standard W4A16 file's size. On Qwen3.5-4B that is an all-6-bit body with 23 of 64 units raised to 8 bits, about 6.8 bits per body weight, in 3,780,277,088 bytes.
  • Blaze-S banks them. It keeps a published 4-bit AutoRound body unchanged, ladders its table and stores its vision tower at 8 bits: 2,598,000,440 bytes, 31% under W4A16.

Drift at equal file size

At GPTQ W4A16's 3,780,662,272 bytes, Blaze-Q is 385 KB smaller and drifts 30× less than GPTQ, 23× less than AutoRound and 8× less than Glaze v2, our earlier export calibrated on in-domain data. Its flip rate of 1.42% is less than half the in-domain export's and below INT8 W8A8's at 5.51 GB. A draft of this work predicted 0.0011 to 0.002 from the error model before any GPU run; the first measurement was 0.0017.

Benchmarks

We ran a 13-task suite in instruct mode with greedy decoding and an 8,192-token cap: text tasks through lm-evaluation-harness on vLLM, vision tasks through VLMEvalKit. Blaze-Q is within one point of BF16 on 12 of 13 tasks, against 3 for GPTQ, 5 for AWQ, 7 for AutoRound and 10 for the in-domain export. Its mean absolute change from BF16 over the 12 accuracy tasks is 0.37 points, against 2.16 for GPTQ. It recovers MATH-500 (83.0 against BF16's 83.4 and GPTQ's 75.8) without any in-domain data. The one task outside the rule, MathVista, moves by −1.1 with a paired interval of −2.6 to +0.5.

On a fixed reasoning panel (a 1,001-question MMLU-Pro subset, MATH-500 and IFEval), every Blaze-Q change from BF16 is inside ±0.4 points and every paired 95% interval includes zero. Against the in-domain export, Blaze-Q is ahead on MMLU-Pro by +2.5 (interval +0.3 to +4.7).

The smaller operating point

The ladder needs nothing else from Blaze. Applied to the published GPTQ, AutoRound and in-domain exports, changing only their vocabulary table, it cuts each by 0.86 GB (23%) and moves excess loss by at most 0.0001. Blaze-S adds an 8-bit vision tower to AutoRound's body and lands at excess loss 0.0418 against AutoRound's 0.0417. On the panel it is at or above AutoRound on every task: MMLU-Pro +1.0 (−0.7 to +2.8), MATH-500 +3.2 (0.0 to +6.4) and IFEval 0.0 (−2.0 to +1.8). Its −7.0 gap to BF16 on MATH-500 belongs to the 4-bit body, not the ladder.

Beyond English

With the English census, the ladder costs code nothing (−0.0003) but costs other scripts: 0.0039 across 11 languages and 0.024 on Thai. Re-ranking the same 65,536 rows by the multilingual census, at identical bytes, halves Blaze-Q's multilingual drift from 0.0051 to 0.0023 and cuts Thai's from 0.028 to 0.0070, for +0.00008 on English benchmark answers. Blaze-Q then drifts 28× less than GPTQ W4A16 across the 11 languages and 15× less on code.

Low drift shows up as accuracy. On MGSM in ten non-English languages, Blaze-Q scores 65.2, equal to BF16 in total (paired change 0.0, −3.2 to +3.0), where GPTQ W4A16 scores 59.0 (−6.2, −9.8 to −2.4). Code is within noise for every model (HumanEval: BF16 82.3, GPTQ 83.5, Blaze-Q 82.9, Blaze-S 85.4; MBPP: 61.5, 63.0, 62.5, 59.0). Blaze-S loses on non-English math like the 4-bit body it keeps (−5.2).

Serving

The body is written as compressed-tensors quantization groups, and the vocabulary as two quantized blocks in tier order plus the index map. The vLLM plugin re-registers Qwen3.5's model class with a tiered table. The LM head runs one Marlin GEMM per block into a single logits buffer. Embedding lookups gather rows directly from the head's Marlin-repacked blocks with a small Triton kernel, bit-identical to vLLM's own lookup at every block shape used, so the table is held on the GPU once; that alone saved 0.41 GB per 4B checkpoint. vLLM 0.29 serves 6-bit groups with its bundled Humming kernel. For GPUs without it, the plugin repacks them as 8-bit words at load, with the same outputs.

A 6-bit body reads about 10% more bytes per decoded token than a 4-bit one. On the A30, Blaze-Q decodes a single stream 3% slower than GPTQ and batches 9% slower, in the same 3.92 GB of GPU memory. Blaze-S reads fewer bytes per token than GPTQ and decodes 16% faster. It leaves 1.49 GiB more KV cache, room for 14.1 concurrent 32k-token requests against 12.7. The extra cache does not shorten long batches: 64 prompts of 16,384 tokens take 114 s in BF16 and about 130 s on every 4- and 6-bit checkpoint, because long prefill is compute-bound and dequantization costs compute.

Building and serving

# Count vocabulary use and plan the ladder (CPU, once per tokenizer)
uv run python scripts/blaze_census.py --answers BF16_ANSWERS.jsonl

# Real export: the laddered table and an 8-bit body (Blaze-Q for Qwen3.5-2B)
python scripts/blaze_real_export.py --model QWEN35_2B_SNAPSHOT --body-bits 8 --output blaze-2b

# Serve on stock vLLM 0.29
pip install -e plugins/blaze_vllm
vllm serve blaze-2b

The plugin loads through vLLM's general-plugins entry point and leaves any other Qwen3.5 checkpoint unchanged. The 4B's 6/8-bit body is GPTQ-rounded by scripts/blaze_gptq.py. Blaze checkpoints are not yet on the Hugging Face Hub.

A second model size

On Qwen3.5-2B the laddered table frees as many bytes as an all-8-bit body costs. Blaze-Q there is the ladder plus every body Linear at 8 bits, rounded to nearest with no calibration data at all: 432 KB under GPTQ W4A16, with excess loss 0.0005 against 0.073. GPTQ loses 12.2 points on MATH-500 and 10.6 on MMLU-Pro; Blaze-Q loses none. Its real format, served by the plugin, drifts as the fake-quantized export did to four decimals.

The ladder is free

With a BF16 body, the ladder adds 0.00022 nats per token and flips 0.40% of tokens against BF16's own 0.35%. Both of its choices matter. Rankings from chat text alone or token-ID order cover 7–8 points less at small K (Figure 2), and 4-bit rows would add 0.26 nats of noise to a busy token's logit.

Why 6 bits, and why 4/8 is not enough

Sensitivity is flat across most of the 4B's depth. The 32 FFNs priced between 0.0017 and 0.0027 nats at 4 bits, except layer 31 (0.0062), and the costliest half of the FFN weights held 65% of their summed price, not the 75% that would let a few 8-bit units do the job. With only 4- and 8-bit kernels, the same budget moves 34 of 64 units from 4 to 8 bits and leaves the other half at its full 4-bit error: excess loss 0.0235, close to half of GPTQ's. A 6-bit option spends the same bytes as +2 bits on every unit, and the error model divides each unit's share by 16; the measured result is 0.0017. When error falls exponentially in bits, spreading them evenly wins.

The vision tower

At exactly W4A16's size with the vision tower in BF16, an all-6-bit body does not fit. The market stepped two FFNs down to 4 bits and drift more than doubled the prediction (0.0071). Storing the vision tower at 8 bits frees 324 MB, enough for the whole body at 6 bits with room to raise 23 units to 8. MMMU moves from −2.9 for the in-domain export to −0.9.

Our own 4-bit GPTQ is weak

At 4 bits our GPTQ (WikiText-2 calibration, no activation ordering) reaches 0.090 against 0.094 for rounding to nearest, where the published GPTQ export reaches 0.054. At 6 bits the same code improves on rounding 1.8× (0.0048 to 0.0027). A first Blaze-S built on our own 4-bit body lost accuracy, so Blaze-S as reported keeps a published body. This is a limitation of our implementation, not of GPTQ.

Limitations

  • One family, two sizes. All results are on Qwen3.5-2B and 4B. The arithmetic predicts large savings for the untied 9B (48% of its export) and other small tied models (Qwen3-1.7B 46%, Llama-3.2-1B 51%, Gemma-3-1B 63%), but none is measured.
  • GPU serving formats only. The baselines are vLLM's GPTQ, AWQ and AutoRound exports, which keep the table in BF16. llama.cpp's k-quant files and MLX already store the vocabulary quantized; Blaze has not been compared against them at equal size.
  • One run each. Every number is one greedy run. Benchmark intervals are paired bootstraps over questions, not over seeds or calibration orders.
  • Suite on the English ladder. The 13-task suite and panel ran on Blaze-Q with the English census as a fake-quantized export (the real checkpoint drifts identically, 0.00169 against 0.00169). The multilingual Blaze-Q moves English drift by +0.00008; its code and multilingual accuracy were measured directly.
  • The yardstick. Comparisons hold file size equal. At equal bits per Linear layer, Blaze's body is plain GPTQ by design, and Blaze-Q reads as many bytes per token as a 6.8-bit body.
  • Long prefill. Quantized checkpoints take about 14% longer than BF16 to serve a batch of long prompts on the A30; Blaze does not change that.
  • Serving needs a plugin tied to vLLM 0.29's Qwen3.5 model, and the 6-bit groups need a kernel for them.

Summary

Four-bit rounding has little left to win; the bytes a 4-bit checkpoint spends on an idle BF16 vocabulary do not. Storing the table by usage is free, and spending its bytes on a 6-bit body brings Qwen3.5-4B to BF16's level at a standard 4-bit file's size, with no training, no Fisher weighting and no in-domain data. The same ladder makes any published 4-bit checkpoint 23% smaller. For small models with large vocabularies, the useful question about a quantized checkpoint is not how many bits each layer gets, but where the file's bytes go.

Next, we want to measure the 9B and the other small tied models, compare against llama.cpp and MLX files at equal size, publish the checkpoints, and report consumer-GPU numbers.
